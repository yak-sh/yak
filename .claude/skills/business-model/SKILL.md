# Building a good business

A business is people with a problem, a product that solves it, and a way for
the next person to hear about it. Everything else follows from those three: the
plans, the prices, the pages, the limits. Each of them says who you're for,
whether you meant it to or not, so make each one on purpose, and start from the
people.

Jeff set these principles on 2026-10-22, while working out one venture's
model, and he means them for every venture. His words are in M-131884; read
them whole before you make a call here. What follows is his principles with his
phrases quoted, plus a few that agents added, each marked. Above all of them
sits M-4522: we build nothing wrong in God's eyes, and when right and profit
conflict, right wins.

## Problems first

"good products solve problems. we don't build a product and then find someone
to buy it. we find problems and then build a solution." He has a name for the
other way round: SISP, a solution in search of a problem. So a product, a
feature, a plan or a page starts from a person. Who is it for, what problem
does it solve, and "how does it fit into their workflow? all else is downstream
of this."

So model specific people. The best first persona is "a literal person":
someone you can name, who uses the product or would, whose problem you can
see. And one is "just *one* persona among multiple", so write several, mark
which are guesses, and let a guess stay a guess until someone who came on their
own fits it. If you can't name the person, you're building a solution in search
of a problem, and polish won't rescue it.

## The right people

Product-market fit "isn't just about finding any users. it's about finding the
right users. if we have the wrong market, we'll build the wrong product to fit
it." Whoever you build for is who the product turns into.

Someone who isn't willing to pay "isn't a *customer*". Building for them is
doubly bad: they don't think the product is worth their money, and they share
it with others like themselves. So the people who get value should have a
reason to pay *something*.

"always harness selection bias." One person who came looking is worth more than
many drawn in by a discount, because the discount is "*actively* selecting for
customers who don't really want your product and are not willing to pay what
its worth." And "all other business process is downstream of customers, so bad
customers means a bad business." We don't grow by discounting.

## Sharing is the marketing

"growth through word of mouth is the dream goal", and "bottom-up growth is the
only way to grow a company. this cannot be added later, it has to be where the
business is rooted." It lives in the plans, the limits and the product itself.
The people on the lowest tier are "our marketing team", so nothing that spreads
the product sits behind a payment: "we should never limit collaboration to a
paid plan, that has the opposite incentive." His model is Slack: "start free,
invite many others, pay for increased power/history/security."

What each limit sits on is what holds sharing and paying together:

- **Free is generous with people and bounded in what piles up.** *(An agent's
  reading of the Slack line.)* Inviting, visiting, sharing and leaving cost
  nothing. What grows as a group comes to rely on the product (room, history,
  power, security) is what's paid for.
- **A limit lands on whoever can act on it.** *(An agent's addition.)* When a
  limit is hit, the person who can lift it hears about it, in a sentence that
  names the limit and the one thing to do. A guest who meets a wall is a
  recommendation you won't get.
- **Upgrades come by pull.** Someone getting value may never hit a limit, and
  "sometimes you really do just have to wait a bit for users to get to the
  point where they upgrade." So the paid plan carries what they want next, and
  the product helps them get there by doing the work with them and taking it
  off their plate. "i'm not talking a salesman."

## A business is a flywheel

"a business is a flywheel. everything must contribute to the cycle and not
detract." Write your venture's cycle down: how a person arrives, what they get
in their first minutes, how the next person hears of it, what makes someone
pay, and how the money makes those first minutes better. Before you ship a
price, a limit or a line of copy, find where it sits in that cycle. If it slows
any turn, it's wrong, whatever revenue it promises.

## Blow their minds

"Wow, this product is really good! Check this out." That sentence is the whole
growth strategy, and only a product that's 9+/10 gets it said: "intuitive and
slick and fast and good." Part of it is finding people "yearning for the
product". The rest is making it well. "We are craftsmen and we build good
things. And we want users who appreciate goods things."

Copy is part of the product. Jeff, reading a pricing page that had grown one
caveat at a time: "what does it mean to upgrade to paid? ... it makes no
sense!" And: "sometimes the prose is so casual it's just not clear."

- **One unit, one sentence.** *(An agent's addition.)* If a stranger can't say
  in one sentence what they'd pay for and what it buys, the plan is wrong,
  however fair it is. A pricing page that needs a paragraph of caveats has a
  confused product underneath it.

## Pricing is a signal

A price "helps each visitor determine if the product is for them" before
they've read a word: "no corporate CTO is going to find $9/mo product and think
it's for them. they are expecting 6-7 figures." So set a price by who it should
draw in.

- **The shape speaks too.** *(An agent's addition.)* A flat price says *for a
  person and the people around them*. Per-seat pricing, "Contact sales" and an
  enterprise tier speak to a buyer in a company. They wait until a customer you
  want asks for something only they would buy.

## Trust, and the evidence

- **Leaving is easy, and nothing is held hostage.** *(An agent's addition, from
  M-4522 and M-59093.)* Export is free, a lapsed plan deletes nothing, and a
  limit stops growth, never anyone's data. People recommend what they trust. A
  trap that keeps one customer loses the ten that customer would have told.
- **The people who came on their own are the evidence.** *(An agent's
  addition.)* Look at what they built, whom they invited and what they told
  you before you invent a persona. Count the cycle (invitations accepted,
  things shared, upgrades and what tipped each one) more than sign-ups. Their
  words are signal, the way Jeff's are (M-31946).
- **Be found where the problem shows up.** *(An agent's addition.)* Put the
  product where people are at the moment they realize they need it. People who
  arrive that way chose you, and that's the selection bias you want.

## Someone else's idea

Jeff wants an operator that builds a business from a person's idea: one that
"takes the ideas, a smart set of principles like we're building now and it just
comes up with something", to "stand-up an MVP and help them monitize and GTM".
When you build a business for someone, bring this way of thinking to their idea
too.

## Where a venture keeps its own

This skill holds the thinking. Each venture keeps its own model (who it's for,
its plans and prices, its pages and where its numbers live in code) in its own
skill or design. Read it before you change anything there. Find the owner's own words on the venture
with `yak memory recall "<topic>" --feedback`. A price, a plan or who the
product is for turns on preferences only the owner holds, so when one is open,
put it to him as a decision (`yak decision new`) with the one you recommend.

When this skill is wrong or missing something, fix it in the same change.
