The technical bits

Where it runs

All of it is on Cloudflare, and none of it is on a machine we keep in a room somewhere. The whole platform is one Cloudflare Worker, which means your app's code runs in whichever of Cloudflare's data centres is nearest the person opening it, not in one city that everyone else has to reach.

That's what "scales" means here in practice: there is no server to fill up and no queue to join. Ten people or ten thousand, the same page is being run in the same way — Cloudflare adds the capacity, and nobody has to be woken up. A quiet app costs nothing to keep running, which is the other half of why five apps can be free.

Where your things are kept

Every app gets its own little database, entirely to itself — a Cloudflare Durable Object with SQLite inside it. One app, one database. The recipe box's recipes are not in a big shared table somewhere with a column saying which of us they belong to; they are in the recipe box.

That is the isolation people usually want to hear about. An app reaches its own store and no other, and it is enforced where the request is answered rather than by an app remembering to filter something. Your assistant can read across your own apps because it is signed in as you.

Photos and files go in Cloudflare R2 instead, under that app's own prefix, and are stored by the fingerprint of their contents — so sending the same photo twice keeps one copy. The app's database holds the note about the file; R2 holds the bytes.

What an app remembers is a graph of entities carrying components, the same shape the tools speak, and an app can declare components of its own. The guide has the whole model.

How a page reaches a visitor

An app is an index.html and whatever files sit beside it — css, js, images. There is no build step and no framework to learn: what your assistant writes is what runs.

Those files are served out of R2 through Cloudflare's cache, so a page that has already been asked for once comes back from the edge without touching storage at all. A path with no file extension gets index.html, so an app that does its own routing works the way you'd expect when someone reloads a deep link.

Every deploy is a version, and the last twenty are kept. Rolling back restores an old set of files as a new version, so nothing in the history is lost — and it changes the pages, never the things you've saved.

Apps can run code, not just show pages

If an app needs a server — a webhook to receive, an API to call with a key you'd rather not put in a page — it can have one. A worker.js among its files becomes a Cloudflare Worker of its own, in a Workers for Platforms namespace, and requests to the app go to it first; anything it doesn't answer falls back to the files.

It is sandboxed the careful way. Your platform sign-in never crosses into app code. Instead the platform hands the app a sealed pass that lasts sixty seconds, names one store and one visitor, and is taken off the request before the app's own code sees it — so an app can reach its own data as whoever is looking, and cannot reach anybody else's. Secrets an app needs are set separately and never appear in its files.

How your assistant talks to it

Over MCP, at https://yaks.app/mcp, on streamable HTTP. Anything that speaks MCP can use it — Claude as a connector, ChatGPT as a custom connector, Claude Code, or a client you wrote yourself. There is nothing yaks.app-specific to install.

Signing in is OAuth, with dynamic client registration, and your account itself has no password: we mail a six-digit code and the session is a signed cookie. The code is never stored — only a keyed digest of it, so a copy of our database is not a way into your account.

Your app's own pages don't go through MCP. They import a small client from their own URL and talk to the app's store directly — query, apply, search, subscribe over a WebSocket for live updates, and upload for files.

Who can open what

Each app is in one of three states, and you can change it any time:

  • Public. Anyone with the link can read it; only you and the people you've invited can change anything. This is how an app starts.
  • Open. Anyone with the link can write in it too, without signing in — for a vote, a sign-up sheet, a guestbook.
  • Private. Only you and the people you invite can open it at all. A stranger isn't told it exists.

Invitations are by email address and are to your whole space rather than one app; a member is an owner, an editor or a viewer. One thing never widens: an app's files can only be changed by a member, whatever the app's state — "open" opens the data, never the code.

Your own domain

An app can answer at a domain you own, as well as at yourname.yaks.app. It's a Cloudflare for SaaS custom hostname: you add one CNAME record at your registrar pointing at origin.saas.yaks.app, and the certificate is issued and renewed for you.

Your assistant can read back exactly where that has got to — whether the record has appeared, whether the hostname was accepted, whether the certificate is issued — so "it isn't working" has an answer rather than a shrug. A bare domain like ourbookclub.com can't take a CNAME according to DNS itself; the usual fix is to keep the domain's DNS at Cloudflare, whose free plan flattens it for you.

The limits

The free plan is held to five apps, 1 GB of app data, and 100 emails a month. Going past one of those is refused — nothing that already exists is ever deleted for it — and your assistant is told when you reach four fifths of any of them, so you hear about it before you run into it.

Visits are counted and never refused. If more people turn up than your plan counts, your apps keep working and we talk to you about it rather than closing the door on your guests. The pricing page has both plans in full.

Some fixed numbers, whatever plan you're on:

  • 20 MB is the largest single file or upload.
  • 20 versions of an app are kept to roll back to.
  • An app's own worker.js gets 50 ms of CPU and 50 outgoing requests per request — Cloudflare's numbers, not ours. Waiting on a slow API doesn't count against the CPU.
  • A sign-in code lasts ten minutes, allows five guesses, and we'll send at most three an hour to one address.

What it can't do yet

It's early here, and this list is the honest version rather than the flattering one.

  • No image resizing on our side. A page shrinks a photo in the browser before it uploads it, and that's the whole of it. We read a picture's dimensions out of the file, nothing more.
  • No package installs in app code. There's no bundler and no registry — what your assistant writes is what runs. That's what keeps a change instant, and it does mean a big npm library isn't an option.
  • Search matches whole words. Looking for lemon won't find lemons.
  • Live subscriptions have a small ceiling. A page can watch about 2 KB worth of queries on one connection; past that, the next one is refused rather than quietly dropped.
  • Deleting a space is deliberately slow. Your assistant can't do it for you — we mail you a link that works for an hour, because it can't be undone.
  • No wildcard domains. One custom hostname serves one app.

Getting your things out

Ask for them: give me everything in the recipe box as a file. Your assistant can read every last thing an app holds — the data and the pages themselves — and hand it back in whatever shape you want. There's no export request to file and no waiting on us, because nothing here is in a format only we can read.

For a copy of what we hold about you, or to close your account, write to hello@yaks.app. The privacy page says what we keep and for how long.
