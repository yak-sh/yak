Unicorn Ideas for Kids: How to Invent One That's Entirely Theirs

2026-06-27 · CrayonBloom

There are two kinds of houses: the ones the unicorn phase has not visited yet, and the ones that have surrendered completely. If you are in the second group, you have noticed the problem. Every unicorn printable on the internet is the same horse with the same horn, and your kid stopped being satisfied by that about two hundred unicorns ago.

The good news is that unicorns are the easiest creature in the world to invent, because nobody can tell your kid they got it wrong.

Five questions that build a unicorn nobody else has

Ask these one at a time, and write down the answers exactly as they come:

  • What is her mane made of? Rainbows is the first answer. Ask what else it could be and you land somewhere stranger, which is where you want to be.
  • What is the horn for? A horn that only stirs soup is a better character than a horn that does magic in general.
  • What does she eat? Expect a strong opinion, firmly held.
  • Where does she sleep? In a meadow, in a cloud, under your kid's actual bed. Somewhere is where the picture happens.
  • What is she doing right now? The last question is the one that turns a creature into a scene, so save it for last and let the others feed it.

You now have a unicorn that exists nowhere else, described in your kid's own words. That is the hard part done.

A dozen scenes to start from

If the questions stall, start from a picture instead and let them argue with it:

  • A unicorn baking a rainbow birthday cake
  • A unicorn foal trotting across a bridge made of clouds
  • A sleepy unicorn curled up under a sky full of stars
  • A unicorn sharing cupcakes with woodland friends
  • A unicorn and a butterfly painting a rainbow across the sky
  • A unicorn carousel spinning at a fair
  • A unicorn splashing through a meadow of giant flowers
  • A unicorn who runs a lemonade stand
  • A unicorn learning to swim, badly
  • A unicorn braiding another unicorn's mane
  • A unicorn delivering the mail
  • A unicorn hiding behind a very small tree

The pattern to notice: each one is a creature, an action, and a place. Hold that shape and almost any idea draws well.

Three small games for after it is colored

The coloring itself buys you twenty quiet minutes. These stretch it:

  • Name her. "What's her name? What's her favorite food?" A page becomes a character, and a character becomes a story you will hear about for a week.
  • Pick a color rule. "This one only gets warm colors." "Every stripe is a different shade." Coloring turns into a small, low-stakes game of choices.
  • Add to the scene. Hand over a blank corner: "What else lives in the meadow?" Butterflies show up, then a sun with a face, then a snack.

None of it has to be structured. A page, a fistful of crayons, and a name for the unicorn is plenty.
